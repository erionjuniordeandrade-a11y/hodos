# Attention networks · orienting, reorienting & neglect

Lesson id: `attention-networks` · Minutes: 18 · Review status: draft

## Opening question

A person omits items on the left during search despite useful hand strength. How would you localize the question?

## Three takeaways

1. Distinguish preparatory orienting from reorienting toward a relevant event. _(step 1: Distinguish preparing attention from redirecting it)_
2. Compare the separate superior-longitudinal courses in a frontoparietal account. _(step 2: Add the parietal–frontal white matter)_
3. Connect the observed spatial bias to a cortical or disconnection hypothesis and individual evidence. _(step 5: Present a right frontoparietal case as a network problem)_

## Steps

### Step 0: Start with the dorsal frontoparietal system

**Claim:** Preparing to look for a target at a chosen location recruits a distributed dorsal attention system. Frontal eye-field and intraparietal regions are useful anatomical anchors on its anterior and posterior sides.

**Sources:**

### Step 1: Distinguish preparing attention from redirecting it

**Claim:** A cue can prepare attention before a target appears. An unexpected relevant target can require reorienting. These are different experimental questions, even when the same person performs both in one trial.

**Sources:**
- **N2** — Corbetta et al., 2000 · voluntary orienting and target detection (PMID 10700263) — functional_measurement
- **N1** — Yeo et al., 2011 · intrinsic cortical network organization (PMID 21653723) — atlas

### Step 3: Separate spatial attention from maintaining a task

**Claim:** Selecting a location, changing a rule and maintaining a task set are related demands, but they are not the same operation. A difficult attention test may recruit more than one control system.

**Sources:**

**Claim:** A rightward error on line bisection is an observation about spatial judgement. Its direction and task conditions matter more than calling the result simply abnormal.

**Sources:**
