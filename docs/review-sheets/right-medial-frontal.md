# Potentials preserved, movement absent (fictional case)

Case id: `right-medial-frontal` · Location: Right medial frontal glioma · Number: 04 · Minutes: N/A (case, not a timed lesson)

## Opening question (conference prompt)

A colleague says: “The potentials were stable to closure, so the corticospinal tract is intact and this must be a stroke.” Which parts of that inference can you support? Name at least two other mechanisms that produce a dense deficit with a preserved monitored pathway, and state what you would examine and order first.

**Vignette:** A 61-year-old adult is discussed on the first morning after resection of a presumed right medial frontal high-grade glioma centred on the supplementary motor region. Before surgery there were focal seizures and a mild slowing of the left hand that improved on dexamethasone. Motor-evoked potentials from the left arm and leg were monitored continuously and remained stable to closure. This morning the patient has a dense left hemiplegia, fails to explore left space at the bedside, and is dysarthric. The fictional postoperative report describes a cavity confined to the supplementary motor region and adjacent white matter, a small area of restricted diffusion under one millilitre away from the descending corridor, and more oedema than on the preoperative FLAIR.

**Known:**
  - Motor-evoked potentials were stable throughout the procedure.
  - The deficit is dense and involves movement, spatial attention and articulation at once.
  - The report describes a small remote restriction and increased oedema; it does not measure the cavity against the cingulate sulcus or the dorsal premotor cortex.

**Unknown:**
  - Whether the patient can move the left limbs automatically, for example when yawning or startled, while unable to move on command.
  - Whether the deficit fluctuates hour to hour, and whether any seizure activity has been recorded.
  - Where the oedema sits relative to the descending, frontoparietal and medial frontal white matter, and whether venous drainage was disturbed.

**Explore:**
  - Compare the medial premotor references with area 4 and follow the descending corridor. The monitored potential samples one projection pathway; it does not measure initiation or the medial frontal contribution to movement.
  - Inspect the connections between medial and lateral frontal cortex on the right. Initiation, articulation and spontaneous movement have medial frontal contributions in both hemispheres.
  - Inspect the right frontoparietal attention references. A right frontal lesion with white-matter oedema can disturb spatial exploration without a parietal lesion.
  - Keep the anterior cingulate and medial frontal cortex in view as a node whose loss impairs initiation across domains, before naming a network.

## Takeaways (debrief titles, in place of a lesson's three takeaways)

1. Say what the monitored pathway can and cannot tell you
2. Read the deficit as a failure of initiation, not one pathway
3. Use the time course and the imaging together, and keep the alternatives testable
4. A defensible response

## Steps (debrief entries)

### Debrief 0: Say what the monitored pathway can and cannot tell you

**Claim:** A stable motor-evoked potential argues against a structural corticospinal lesion under the conditions of monitoring at the time of recording. It says nothing about initiation, about the medial frontal contribution to movement, about spatial attention, or about oedema that develops after closure. In a large peri-Rolandic series, most early postoperative motor deficits occurred without any change in the potentials, and most of those recovered. Preserved potentials therefore do not make the deficit a stroke by exclusion; they narrow the structural hypothesis and leave the network hypothesis open.

**Sources:**
- **mep** — Giampiccolo et al. · Long-term motor deficit in brain tumour surgery with preserved intra-operative motor-evoked potentials (PMID 33615216)

### Debrief 1: Read the deficit as a failure of initiation, not one pathway

**Claim:** Contralateral akinesia that eases with automatic or emotional movement, reduced spontaneous speech or dysarthria, and, on the right, neglect are the described consequences of removing the supplementary motor region. The facilitation sign is the bedside finding that separates initiation failure from paralysis. A right medial frontal cavity inside a larger zone of white-matter oedema can involve descending, frontoparietal and medial frontal fibres together, which is why three symptoms can arise and recover as one.

**Sources:**
- **sma** — Krainik et al. · Role of the supplementary motor area in motor deficit following medial frontal lobe surgery (PMID 11552019)
- **sma-series** — Bannur & Rajshekhar · Post operative supplementary motor area syndrome: clinical features and outcome (PMID 10912196)
- **neglect** — He et al. · Breakdown of functional connectivity in frontoparietal networks underlies behavioral deficits in spatial neglect (PMID 17359924)

### Debrief 2: Use the time course and the imaging together, and keep the alternatives testable

**Claim:** A remote restriction under one millilitre cannot carry a dense hemiplegia. Multi-domain improvement within days of restarting dexamethasone, in the window in which its effect on peritumoural water is measurable, favours a reversible oedema-related mechanism. That reading is a hypothesis with consequences: examine for facilitation at every visit; if the level fluctuates from hour to hour rather than within an examination, obtain prolonged EEG for non-convulsive seizures; if recovery stalls, image the venous system; measure the cavity against the cingulate sulcus and the dorsal premotor cortex, because extension into those territories is what predicted long-term deficit in the series cited, not the resection volume. Do not taper the steroid until the examination plateaus, and assess recovery at three months with timed hand tasks and a neglect battery, not with a strength grade alone.

**Sources:**
- **steroid** — Bastin et al. · Effects of dexamethasone on cerebral perfusion and water diffusion in patients with high-grade glioma (PMID 16484419)
- **mep** — Giampiccolo et al. · Long-term motor deficit in brain tumour surgery with preserved intra-operative motor-evoked potentials (PMID 33615216)

### Debrief 3: A defensible response

**Claim:** “The stable potentials make a structural corticospinal lesion unlikely, but they do not explain the deficit. Dense hemiplegia with facilitation, neglect and dysarthria after a right supplementary motor resection reads as an initiation failure of the medial frontal region within a larger, steroid-responsive white-matter lesion. I would document facilitation and a neglect battery today, keep the steroid until the examination plateaus, obtain prolonged EEG if the level fluctuates between examinations, measure the cavity against the cingulate and premotor cortex, and tell the family that recovery over weeks is expected but not guaranteed.” This is an example of reasoning, not the only acceptable wording.
